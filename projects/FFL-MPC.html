Constrained Nonlinear Control via Filtered Feedback Linearization and MPC

A robust control framework for nonlinear MIMO systems subject to unknown, unmeasured disturbances and actuator constraints.

Overview

This project develops a hybrid Filtered Feedback Linearization–Model Predictive Control (FFL–MPC) framework for control-affine nonlinear systems affected by unknown and unmeasured disturbances. The objective is to retain the disturbance-rejection capability of filtered inversion while also satisfying prescribed actuator limits.

The key difficulty is that FFL generates the actual control command through low-pass filter dynamics, whereas MPC optimizes a virtual input. The project therefore develops a dynamic constraint-mapping method that transforms bounds on the physical input—and, when required, its derivatives— into admissible constraints on the MPC virtual input.

Main idea: use FFL to remove the explicit dependence of the controller on unknown disturbances, use MPC to determine the virtual input, and use the dynamics of the FFL filter to map actuator constraints into time-varying feasible bounds for the MPC.

Nonlinear System & Control Challenge

Consider a control-affine nonlinear MIMO system

\[ \dot{x}=f(x)+G(x)u+D(x)w, \qquad y=h(x), \]

where \(u\) is the physical control input and \(w\) represents unknown, unmeasured disturbances. After differentiating each output according to its relative degree, the input-output dynamics can be written compactly as

\[ y^{(r)} = \Lambda(x)+\Gamma(x)u+\Phi(x,\zeta_w). \]

Standard Feedback Linearization would require the disturbance-dependent term \(\Phi(x,\zeta_w)\), which is unavailable in practice. In addition, a conventional FL virtual-input law does not directly account for actuator constraints. The proposed architecture addresses these two issues separately but within a unified controller.

1. Filtered Feedback Linearization

In FFL, the ideal Feedback Linearization command \(u^{*}\) is passed through a stable low-pass filter,

\[ \eta_z(p)u=\eta_z(0)u^{*}, \]

where \(z\) is a filter-design parameter. By combining the filter dynamics with the nonlinear input-output relation, the implementable FFL command can be expressed as

\[ [\,\eta_z(p)-\eta_z(0)\,]u = \eta_z(0)\Gamma^{-1}(x) \left[\nu-y^{(r)}\right]. \]

Importantly, the unknown disturbance term \(\Phi(x,\zeta_w)\) and the nonlinear drift term \(\Lambda(x)\) no longer appear explicitly in this expression. The controller instead requires the relative-degree information, the dynamic-inversion matrix \(\Gamma(x)\), and output derivatives. This provides the robustness advantage of the filtered formulation while reducing the model information needed for implementation.

For sufficiently large filter gain \(z\), the residual effect of the filter dynamics becomes small and the input-output behavior approaches \(y_i^{(r_i)}\approx\nu_i\). This simple relationship is used as the prediction model for MPC.

2. Model Predictive Control

Each controlled output is represented inside the MPC by an integrator chain corresponding to its relative degree. The resulting prediction model is independent of the complete nonlinear plant model:

\[ x_{\mathrm{mpc}}(k+1) = \bar{A}_{\mathrm{mpc}}x_{\mathrm{mpc}}(k) + \bar{B}_{\mathrm{mpc}}\nu(k), \qquad y_{\mathrm{mpc}}(k) = \bar{C}_{\mathrm{mpc}}x_{\mathrm{mpc}}(k). \]

MPC then selects the virtual command \(\nu\) by minimizing a tracking and control-effort objective over the prediction horizon. However, the actuator limits apply to \(u\), not directly to \(\nu\). This motivates the central contribution of the project: dynamic constraint mapping.

3. Dynamic Constraint Mapping

The FFL filter is treated as a dynamic system by defining an input-dynamics state containing the physical input and its derivatives:

\[ U = \begin{bmatrix} u^{\top} & (pu)^{\top} & \cdots & (p^{\rho-1}u)^{\top} \end{bmatrix}^{\top}. \]

The filter dynamics can then be represented in discrete time as

\[ U(k+1) = A_{ud}U(k) + B_{ud} \left[-y^{(r)}(k)+\nu(k)\right]. \]

Because the relative degree between \(\nu\) and each component of \(U\) is known, prescribed bounds on \(u\), \(pu\), ..., \(p^{\rho-1}u\) can be propagated through these dynamics and rewritten as linear inequalities on the virtual input. The overall admissible virtual-input set is the intersection of the feasible sets generated by the imposed physical bounds:

\[ \mathcal{V}(k) = \bigcap_{i=1}^{\rho}\mathcal{V}_i(k). \]
Why this matters: the physical actuator limits may be constant, but their equivalent bounds in the virtual-input space are generally time-varying. MPC therefore operates inside a feasible region that evolves with the FFL input dynamics.

Case Study: Three Coupled Pendulums

The framework is evaluated on a nonlinear MIMO system consisting of three pendulums interconnected by springs. Control torques are applied to the first and third pendulums, while independent unknown disturbance torques act on all three pendulums. The controlled outputs are the angular positions of the first and third pendulums.

Both outputs have relative degree \(r_1=r_2=2\). The simulations use a sampling time of \(T_s=1\) ms and the second-order filter \(\eta_z(s)=(s+z)^2\). The reference trajectories contain multiple sinusoidal components, while the system is subjected to independent zero-mean Gaussian disturbances.

Disturbance Rejection & Filter Gain

The first numerical example investigates disturbance rejection without imposing actuator constraints. Three filter gains, \(z=25\), \(50\), and \(100\), are compared. Increasing the filter gain progressively improves the approximation of the ideal input-output dynamics and therefore improves tracking performance.

z = 25 Noticeable tracking error
z = 50 Improved tracking
z = 100 Best disturbance rejection

At \(z=100\), the outputs track the reference trajectories closely despite the external disturbances, while the control inputs exhibit smaller fluctuations than for the lower filter gains.

Input-Constrained Control

The second example evaluates the constraint-mapping algorithm using \(z=100\) and actuator limits \(-4.25\leq u_1\leq4.25\) and \(-4.25\leq u_2\leq4.25\) N·m. Without constraint mapping, the same controller can demand inputs beyond these limits. With the proposed mapping, both physical control inputs remain within the prescribed bounds throughout the simulation.

When an actuator reaches its boundary, the corresponding virtual input simultaneously reaches its mapped boundary. Temporary tracking degradation occurs during these intervals because perfect tracking would require control effort beyond the available actuator range. Once the required effort returns to the feasible range, tracking recovers.

Geometric Interpretation

The constraint mapping can also be visualized geometrically. The actuator constraints define a constant feasible region in the \((u_1,u_2)\) space. In contrast, the corresponding feasible region in the \((\nu_1,\nu_2)\) space changes continuously with time as it is generated from the input dynamics.

The physical and virtual trajectories remain inside their respective feasible regions. When the actual input reaches a boundary—or an edge formed by two simultaneous actuator limits—the virtual-input trajectory reaches the corresponding boundary of its mapped feasible region at the same instant.

Key Contributions & Takeaways

  • A hybrid FFL–MPC architecture for nonlinear MIMO systems with unknown disturbances and actuator constraints.
  • FFL removes the explicit dependence of the implementable controller on the unknown disturbance term and nonlinear drift dynamics.
  • A dynamic constraint-mapping algorithm converts bounds on the actual control input and its derivatives into feasible constraints on the MPC virtual input.
  • The numerical study demonstrates robust tracking under disturbances and successful enforcement of actuator limits, including intervals where the unconstrained controller would exceed the admissible range.
