Hierarchical Traffic Flow Control for Heterogeneous Freeways

Distributed multivariable extremum seeking and filtered feedback linearization for congestion management in mixed autonomous and human-driven traffic.

Overview

This project develops a two-level hierarchical control framework for heterogeneous freeways where autonomous vehicles (AVs) and human-driven vehicles (HVs) share the roadway. The objective is to increase traffic flow, prevent congestion from propagating upstream, and regulate the densities of both vehicle classes without relying on a detailed traffic model.

At the upper level, Distributed Multivariable Extremum Seeking (DMV-ES) searches online for desirable AV and HV densities. At the lower level, Distributed Filtered Feedback Linearization (D-FFL) converts those density references into recommended vehicle speeds while providing robustness to uncertain and unmeasured traffic dynamics.

Main idea: separate traffic optimization from density regulation: DMV-ES determines where the traffic densities should operate, while D-FFL determines how vehicle speeds should be adjusted to reach those densities.

Heterogeneous Traffic Model

The freeway is divided into cells and modeled using an enhanced multi-class METANET formulation. Each cell contains separate density and average-velocity states for AVs and HVs:

\[ x_{i,A}= \begin{bmatrix} \rho_{i,A} & v_{i,A} \end{bmatrix}^{\top}, \qquad x_{i,H}= \begin{bmatrix} \rho_{i,H} & v_{i,H} \end{bmatrix}^{\top}. \]

The density dynamics follow conservation of vehicles, while the velocity dynamics include relaxation, convection, downstream density effects, and disturbances. Unlike a conventional fixed-parameter model, the formulation introduces state-dependent parameters and dynamic coupling terms to better represent the interaction between the two vehicle classes.

The coupling is interpreted through dynamically allocated road-space fractions \(\alpha_{i,A}\) and \(\alpha_{i,H}\), satisfying

\[ \alpha_{i,A}+\alpha_{i,H}=1. \]

These fractions change according to the traffic regime—free flow, semi-congested, or congested—and allow the achievable total traffic flow to depend jointly on the densities of AVs and HVs.

Hierarchical Control Architecture

Traffic management is formulated as a distributed hierarchy. Each controlled region locally optimizes its traffic condition while accounting for interaction with neighboring cells.

1. Distributed Multivariable Extremum Seeking

The upper-level controller determines the desired density pair \(\rho_i^{*}=[\rho_{i,A}^{*},\rho_{i,H}^{*}]^{\top}\) for each controlled cell without requiring an explicit analytical model of the traffic-flow optimum.

The local objective balances two goals: maximizing the traffic flow in the target cell and reducing large flow differences between neighboring cells:

\[ J_i = w_{i,1}Q_i^{2} - w_{i,2}\left(Q_i-Q_{i-1}\right)^{2}. \]

Small periodic perturbations are applied independently to the AV and HV density references. By filtering and demodulating the resulting objective signal, DMV-ES estimates the local gradient and continuously updates the density references toward the flow-maximizing operating point.

Why multivariable ES? AV and HV densities are coupled. Optimizing them jointly allows the controller to account for how changing one vehicle class affects the traffic conditions experienced by the other.

2. Distributed Filtered Feedback Linearization

The lower-level controller receives the desired densities from DMV-ES and regulates the actual AV and HV densities by modifying the recommended velocities communicated to the vehicles.

For each vehicle class, the relative degree between density and the local speed-control command is two. The density dynamics can therefore be written in the input-output form

\[ \ddot{\rho} = \Phi+\varphi u, \]

Direct Feedback Linearization would require complete knowledge of the nonlinear traffic dynamics and disturbances. D-FFL instead passes the ideal inversion command through stable filter dynamics, producing an implementable controller that requires substantially less model information and does not require explicit knowledge of the unknown disturbances.

Case Study: I-485 Freeway, Charlotte

The framework is evaluated using traffic data from approximately 10 miles of I-485 in Charlotte, North Carolina, between Mallard Creek Road and Harrisburg Road. The freeway is divided into ten one-mile cells, with several cells experiencing congestion during the selected peak-hour traffic period.

The METANET parameters are calibrated using a corresponding PTV VISSIM traffic environment. The simulations also account for different communication capabilities: AV speed recommendations are updated substantially faster than the speed information communicated to human-driven vehicles.

Congestion Management Results

Without active control, congestion propagates upstream and the densities in the affected cells move toward more severe traffic conditions. With the DMV-ES–D-FFL framework, the upper-level controller identifies improved density references and the lower-level controller tracks them, preventing the target cells from approaching jam conditions.

The resulting traffic flow increases across the controlled region while the objective function improves. Compared with a distributed mainstream traffic controller based on an adaptive PID scheme, the proposed framework reaches the target densities more rapidly and dissipates congestion more effectively.

MATLAB–VISSIM Validation

A second study evaluates the controller using a direct MATLAB–VISSIM co-simulation. Traffic densities generated by the microscopic VISSIM simulation are transferred to MATLAB/Simulink, where DMV-ES updates the desired AV/HV densities and D-FFL computes the corresponding control commands. The recommended velocities are then sent back to VISSIM.

The VISSIM scenario uses a mixed fleet of 60% AVs and 40% HVs. AVs receive speed information approximately three times more frequently than HVs, representing the higher communication capability available to connected autonomous vehicles. The co-simulation results again show lower densities in congested cells and improved traffic flow relative to the uncontrolled case.

Key Contributions & Takeaways

  • An enhanced multi-class METANET model with state-dependent parameters and dynamic AV/HV coupling.
  • A two-level distributed architecture combining model-free density optimization through DMV-ES with robust nonlinear density regulation through D-FFL.
  • Explicit consideration of AV–HV interactions and different communication rates between infrastructure and the two vehicle classes.
  • Validation using both macroscopic traffic simulations and a direct MATLAB–PTV VISSIM co-simulation based on a real I-485 freeway corridor.
